Alignment does not end when a model is deployed.
— SPEAR treats it as something people and AI keep negotiating, maintaining, and repairing in use.

01 / position

1/ From a Pipeline to an Interaction

Alignment is often framed as a pre-deployment optimization problem: collect human feedback, learn preferences or principles, fine-tune, and deploy. That has driven major progress, but it under-specifies what happens once AI systems act in situated, long-term, and social contexts.

alignment as a pipeline
collect human feedback→learn an objective→fine-tune→deploy “aligned” system
alignment as interaction
develop→maintain→repair↺shared understanding · appropriate agency · calibrated reliance
Why the pipeline is not enough
Intent is rarely complete, stable, or fully specifiable in advance
  • Users may not know exactly what they want until they see what the system produces.
  • They refine goals across turns, discover new uses, change preferences, and form new expectations as AI enters everyday work.
  • Alignment is bidirectional: AI adapts to people, and people adapt to AI, updating mental models, trust, and behavior.
An HCI definition of interactive human-AI alignment

Alignment is the ongoing, interactive process through which humans and AI systems develop, maintain, and repair shared understanding, appropriate agency, and calibrated reliance over time.

Summary

Alignment cannot be understood only as a property encoded in model weights before deployment. It emerges through interaction after deployment, which makes it an interaction design problem.

02 / five principles

2/ SPEAR: Five Pillars of Interactive Alignment

SPEAR asks five interactive alignment questions. The first three, adopted from Terry et al., form the core loop within a task. The last two extend that loop across tasks and beyond sessions.

Summary

Each principle names a point where alignment can succeed or fail after deployment, and each has a concrete interaction that makes it negotiable instead of implicit.

03 / bridging HCI and alignment

3/ Interaction Mechanisms as Alignment Mechanisms

These questions are not entirely new. HCI has studied closely related problems for decades. What is new is the scale, opacity, agency, and adaptivity of contemporary AI systems, which makes those theories newly relevant to alignment.

HCI foundations for each principle
Old theories, new alignment problems
SSpecification
Grounding & repair
Communication needs common ground, built through clarification, acknowledgment, evidence, and repair of misunderstandings.
Clark & Brennan 1991; Brennan 1998
PProcess
Distributed cognition
Cognitive work is spread across people, tools, and representations; agents now take on part of it.
Hutchins & Klausen 1996; Hollan, Hutchins & Kirsh 2000
Mixed-initiative interaction
A system decides when to act, ask, defer, or pause, based on its model of the user’s goal.
Horvitz 1999
Activity theory
Tool-mediated work is organized by purposes, rules, community, and a division of labor, so delegation raises who may decide what.
Nardi 1996; Engeström 2000
EEvaluation
Gulfs of execution & evaluation
The gap between what users want to do and the actions a system offers, and between what it shows and whether the goal was met.
Hutchins, Hollan & Norman 1985
Human-AI interaction guidelines
Make clear what the system can do, how well, and why it did what it did; support efficient correction.
Amershi et al. 2019
Why human-AI interaction is hard
Capability uncertainty and output complexity make AI behavior hard to anticipate and judge.
Yang et al. 2020
Agent visibility
Agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logs expose what an agent is doing and has done.
Chan et al. 2024
AAdaptation
Adaptation & appropriation
People repurpose technologies and develop new practices through use, beyond what designers intended.
Moran 2002; Dourish 2003; Dix 2007
Structuration theory
Rules and resources both shape action and are reproduced through it; a technology’s structure is enacted in recurring use.
Giddens 2014; Stones 2017; Orlikowski 2000
RRecalibration
Computers as social actors
People apply social rules and expectations to computers automatically.
Nass, Steuer & Tauber 1994
Groupware & social dynamics
People adapt their coordination practices to technology-mediated work.
Grudin 1994
Intentional stance
We predict a system by attributing beliefs and desires to it, whether or not it has them.
Dennett 1989

Takeaways

  • Treat alignment as an ongoing, interactive process, not only a pre-deployment property.
  • Design for all five questions: specification, process, evaluation, adaptation, and recalibration.
  • Support informed, revisable calibration rather than assuming more trust, less checking, or broader delegation is always better.
